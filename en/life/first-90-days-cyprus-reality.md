> **Short answer:** Your first 90 days in Cyprus revolve around four tasks: finding housing, opening a bank account, getting your residence permit, and surviving culture shock. Budget EUR 5,000-8,000 per person for initial setup.


Every expat blog tells you Cyprus is paradise. Sunshine, low taxes, friendly locals. What they skip is the part where you spend three hours at the post office trying to activate a SIM card, or the afternoon you discover every shop within walking distance is closed until 4pm. This guide covers what actually happens in your first 90 days — the logistics, the emotions, and the things nobody warned you about.

> **This article is based on:** accounts from expat communities (Telegram, Facebook groups), government procedural timelines from [gov.cy](https://www.gov.cy/), and the experiences of dozens of English-speaking expats who relocated between 2023 and 2026.

## What happens in weeks 1-2?

Your first two weeks are about survival logistics. You are jet-lagged or road-weary, everything looks unfamiliar, and you are about to learn that Cyprus operates on its own clock.

### What catches people off-guard immediately

- **Siesta is real.** Most small shops, pharmacies, and government offices close between 13:00 and 16:00 (sometimes 15:00–17:00 depending on the season). Supermarkets and chain stores stay open, but local businesses do not. Plan errands for morning.
- **Sunday is dead.** Almost everything is closed except restaurants and petrol stations. Even some supermarkets close by 14:00 on Sundays. This is not "reduced hours" — it is closed.
- **Left-hand driving** (if you are coming from the US, Canada, or continental Europe). Cyprus drives on the left, British-style. Roundabouts, road signs, and lane discipline will feel unfamiliar. Rental cars have right-hand-drive steering.
- **Noise.** Construction starts at 7:00am (legally). Dogs bark. Neighbours socialise loudly on balconies well past midnight. Cyprus is not a quiet country.
- **Water.** Tap water is safe in most cities but tastes heavily of chlorine. Most expats switch to bottled or filtered water immediately. Budget €15–20/month for 19-litre bottles from delivery services.

### Week 1–2 checklist

- [ ] Find temporary housing (Airbnb, short-term rental) — give yourself at least 2–4 weeks before signing a long lease
- [ ] Get a local SIM card — Cyta, Epic, or PrimeTel. You need a passport. Buy at a physical store, not a kiosk, for proper activation. Expect €10–15 for a prepaid SIM with data.
- [ ] Open a basic bank account — Bank of Cyprus and Eurobank Limited (formerly Hellenic Bank) are the main options. Bring your passport, proof of address (even a hotel booking may work temporarily), and proof of income or employment. Expect the process to take 1–3 weeks due to KYC compliance checks. See our [bank account guide](/en/business/bank-account-cyprus-foreigners/).
- [ ] Register for an ARC (Alien Registration Certificate) appointment through the [Migration Department](https://www.gov.cy/mip-md/en/) — wait times vary depending on your permit type and the office location
- [ ] Download Bolt and/or FreeNow for transport if you do not have a car yet
- [ ] Find a supermarket and a laiki (farmers' market) near you

> **Important:** Do not sign a 12-month lease in Week 1. Neighbourhoods feel completely different after living in them for two weeks. Rent short-term first, explore, then commit. See our [Limassol neighbourhood guide](/en/life/best-neighborhoods-limassol/) for area-by-area breakdowns.

## What happens in weeks 3-4?

This is the phase where excitement meets paperwork. You need to start converting your temporary existence into an official one.

### Key bureaucratic tasks

**TIN (Tax Identification Number):** Apply at the [Tax Department](https://www.gov.cy/mof/en/). You need a passport copy, proof of address, and your employment contract or company registration. Processing takes 1–3 weeks. You cannot open some bank accounts or sign certain contracts without a TIN.

**ARC / Residence Permit:** Your appointment was (hopefully) booked in Week 1. The actual appointment involves biometrics, document submission, and a fee (€40–70 depending on permit type). After the appointment, expect 4–8 weeks for the physical card. You will receive a receipt that serves as temporary proof of application.

**Bank account (continued):** If you started the process in Week 1, this is when compliance checks finish and your account actually becomes usable. Be patient — Cypriot banks are thorough with KYC (Know Your Customer) requirements, especially for non-EU nationals.

**Driving licence:** If you hold a UK licence, you can drive in Cyprus for up to 6 months. After that, you must exchange it. EU licence holders can use theirs indefinitely. US, Canadian, and Australian licences require an exchange or a new Cyprus test — see our [driving licence guide](/en/life/driving-license-cyprus/).

**Utility connections:** Electricity (EAC), water (local water board), and internet (Cyta, PrimeTel, Cablenet, Epic). Expect deposits: €150–300 for electricity, €100–200 for water. Internet installation takes 3–10 business days. See our [utilities guide](/en/life/utilities-cyprus/).

### Emotional state: frustration is normal

Around Week 3–4, the honeymoon wears off. Things that seemed charming (the relaxed pace, the "tomorrow" attitude) start to feel like obstacles. Government offices have confusing procedures. Bank staff may seem unhurried. Nothing happens as fast as you expect.

This is textbook culture shock. It is temporary.

## What happens in month 2?

By Week 5–8, you start developing routines. You know which supermarket has what, which coffee shop has decent wifi, and which road to avoid at 8am.

### What typically clicks in Month 2

- **Local shops vs supermarkets.** You discover the butcher is cheaper and better than the supermarket meat counter. The bakery around the corner sells fresh bread for €1. The laiki market on Wednesday morning becomes a weekly ritual.
- **Driving patterns.** You learn that the motorway between Limassol and Paphos is fine, but Nicosia ring road at 7:30am is a parking lot. You discover shortcuts through residential streets. You stop flinching at roundabouts.
- **Social circles start forming.** You find a coworking space, join a gym, attend a Telegram meetup. People you met in Week 2 become actual friends. This is the critical period for building your social foundation.
- **Administrative tasks resolve.** Bank account works. TIN arrives. Internet is connected. The stress of setting up from scratch starts to fade.

### How to find your tribe

Community is what determines whether you stay or leave. Here are the main channels:

- **Telegram groups** — city-specific expat chats are the most active: [Expats Cyprus](https://t.me/expatscy), Limassol expats, Paphos expats. These are where people ask real questions and get real answers.
- **Facebook groups** — "Expats in Cyprus," "British Expats in Paphos," "Americans in Cyprus." Larger groups, slower response times, but useful for housing and services recommendations.
- **Coworking spaces** — Limassol has several (The Base, Sandbox, Work Island). Paphos has fewer options but the community is tighter. Good for remote workers.
- **Sports clubs** — running clubs, cycling groups, padel courts, yoga studios. Physical activity is one of the fastest ways to build connections.
- **Language classes** — Greek language courses at the Cyprus University of Technology (Limassol) or private tutors. Even basic Greek opens doors. See our [learning Greek guide](/en/life/learning-greek-language-cyprus/).
- **Religious communities** — if applicable. Anglican churches in Paphos and Limassol have active English-speaking congregations.

## What happens in month 3?

Around the 90-day mark, expats tend to split into two groups. One group has found their routine, has a social circle forming, and is starting to feel at home. The other group is lonely, frustrated with bureaucracy, and questioning whether they made the right decision.

### Signs you are integrating well

- You have at least 2–3 people you could call for help
- You have a GP registered under GESY (if eligible)
- You know your neighbourhood by name, not just by GPS
- You have stopped mentally converting every price to your home currency
- You have a favourite coffee shop where the staff recognise you

### Signs you may be struggling

- You spend most evenings alone
- You are comparing everything unfavourably to your home country
- You avoid leaving the house because errands feel overwhelming
- You have not made a single local friend (Cypriot or expat)
- You are counting down days rather than settling in

> **Important:** If isolation persists beyond Month 3, it is worth seeking support. Culture shock can tip into genuine depression. See the mental health section in our [culture shock guide](/en/life/culture-shock-cyprus-integration/) for English-speaking therapist resources in Cyprus.

## What does the emotional curve look like?

Almost every expat goes through these phases. Knowing the pattern helps you ride it out:

1. **Excitement** (Week 1–3) — everything is new, the weather is amazing, you feel brave and adventurous
2. **Frustration** (Week 3–6) — bureaucracy, language barriers, things not working the way you expect
3. **Adjustment** (Week 6–10) — you develop workarounds, routines form, frustration fades
4. **Acceptance** (Week 10+) — Cyprus is not perfect, but it is home. You stop fighting the culture and start working with it

The dip in Phase 2 is where people give up. If you push through to Phase 3, it almost always gets better.

## What do experienced expats wish they had known?

These are the most frequently cited regrets and surprises from English-speaking expat communities:

- **Bring more documents than you think you need.** Apostilled birth certificates, marriage certificates, university degrees — you will need originals for various applications. Getting these from abroad after you move is slow and expensive.
- **Utility deposits are steep.** Budget €500–800 for electricity, water, and internet deposits combined. This is separate from your rent deposit.
- **The healthcare system works, but differently.** GESY is free at point of use (for eligible residents), but the GP gatekeeping model is different from what Americans or Canadians are used to. See our [GESY guide](/en/life/healthcare-cyprus-gesy/).
- **Summer is genuinely brutal.** July and August are 38–42°C. Air conditioning is not optional — it is a survival necessity. Budget for summer electricity bills that are 2–3x winter levels.
- **Water shortages happen.** Cyprus has periodic water supply restrictions in summer, particularly in rural areas. Keep reserves.
- **"Tomorrow" really does mean eventually.** Deliveries, repairs, government processing — recalibrate your expectations by a factor of 2–3x compared to Northern Europe or North America.

## What are the frequently asked questions?

**How much money should I bring for the first 3 months in Cyprus?**
At least €5,000–8,000 per person for deposits (rent, utilities), first month's rent, food, transport, and SIM. For a family, budget €10,000–15,000. Your bank account may take 1–3 weeks to open.

**Do I need a car in Cyprus?**
Almost certainly. There is no metro, no rail network, and bus routes are limited. Bolt/taxis work for the first weeks, but most expats buy or rent a car within the first month.

**How long does the residence permit process take?**
Migration Department appointment timing varies by district and permit route. After the appointment, physical-card timing also varies. You receive a receipt as temporary proof immediately.

**What is the biggest surprise for new expats?**
The siesta (everything closes 13:00–16:00), the noise (construction from 7am), and how long bureaucratic processes take. Budget 2–3x the time you expect for everything.
