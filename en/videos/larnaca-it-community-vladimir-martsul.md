For expats in Larnaca, a regular informal meeting can become a starting point for friendships and shared projects. In this full interview, published on 17 June 2024, developer Vladimir Martsul explains how Larnaca IT began and why he wants it to stay focused on IT and related interests.

The story includes successes and limits: people meet and form smaller groups, while a programme of technical talks proves harder to sustain. It is an account of the community at the time of recording, not a current events calendar.

## Why organise something in Larnaca?

Vladimir had lived in Cyprus for almost three years at the time of the interview. He remembers a period when there were fewer IT people and fewer relevant events in Larnaca. He travelled to Limassol for gatherings, but doing so regularly meant time on the road and awkward journeys home in the evening.

As more people arrived, he and a colleague saw a simpler option: invite people locally to drink coffee or beer and talk. The first announcement went into an existing Larnaca chat, with a place and an invitation. Vladimir recalls roughly ten people at the first meeting.

## From Mackenzie beach to a rooftop bar

The first meetings were at Mackenzie beach, known for low-flying aircraft. A comfortable setting and enough light to see each other were part of the choice. When autumn became cooler, Vladimir contacted a new rooftop bar in the city centre and spoke with its Russian-speaking manager.

The meetings moved there and, he says, had continued for almost two years by the interview. The video description names Memories Rooftop Bar. That records the venue associated with the interview; it does not confirm today’s location.

## How a general meeting creates smaller circles

Vladimir says people who met at the gatherings went on to form smaller circles around their interests. He mentions social activities and mothers who got to know one another and then arranged their own meetings. He does not claim to run every group or to solve questions about schools and childcare.

The event is announced as IT or IT-adjacent, but people from other fields also come. Designers and marketing professionals are among the examples. Keeping a recognisable theme makes it easier for someone to decide whether the meeting is for them.

## Why technical talks were harder to maintain

Vladimir had organised two meetups with talks. Finding initial topics and a place worked, and he felt those events went well. Continuing was harder: potential speakers were busy, interest faded and discussions of possible talks did not become a regular programme.

Katerina suggests a lighter format where ordinary developers discuss something useful without the expectations of a major conference. Vladimir is open to trying. The conversation does not announce that such a recurring programme has already been established.

## A noncommercial project with practical benefits

Vladimir says he does not receive money from the bar for bringing people and does not seek to make the community commercial. He gains useful contacts, including some that help with work, and enjoys seeing others spend time together.

Most newcomers arrive through friends and colleagues. Social posts, personal reminders and word of mouth do much of the work. He is open to sponsorship but does not want to finance advertising himself. One potential sponsor had expressed interest and then disappeared; the interview does not establish a successful sponsorship deal.

## Why he does not want to gather everyone

Asked about broadening the community to all Russian-speaking residents of Larnaca, Vladimir says he does not want to spread himself too thin. His interest is IT and nearby fields, with smaller projects and friendships free to grow from those encounters.
