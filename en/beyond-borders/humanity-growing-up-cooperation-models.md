In March 2026 a Shahed-type drone struck a fuel depot beside Dubai International Airport — [the world's busiest international hub](https://www.washingtonpost.com/world/2026/03/16/dubai-airport-drone-strike-iran/), handling over 90 million passengers a year. The fourth drone incident in a short period. 300+ flights disrupted, 291 routes across 110 countries affected. Within a week [9,500+ flights cancelled](https://blog.gettransport.com/news/global-airline-cancellations-impact-logistics/) across all Gulf hubs.

If you are reading this in Cyprus — or anywhere in the eastern Mediterranean — you know what it feels like to be caught inside a systemic rupture. In 2022, when the sanctions regime against Russia landed overnight, tens of thousands of British expats with business ties to Moscow, property in Dubai, or clients across the Commonwealth found their arrangements suddenly scrambled. Not because they were party to a conflict, but because the systems they depended on — financial, logistical, legal — became transmission points for geopolitical stress. Understanding [how Cyprus's tax system is changing in 2026](/en/business/cyprus-tax-reform-2026/) matters precisely because these systemic shifts reshape daily life for expats.

The Dubai disruption is the same pattern, scaled up. And it is not a story about heroes and villains. It is a **systemic pattern** that repeats at civilisational level.

This piece is an attempt to understand: why does the pattern reproduce, which alternative models have actually worked in history, and what do developmental psychology, systems thinking, and game theory say about whether humanity can "grow up."

## 1. The Pattern: Dubai as Symptom

### The Facts

16 March 2026: a [Shahed-type drone struck a fuel depot](https://www.aljazeera.com/news/2026/3/16/drone-strike-disrupts-dubai-flights-as-iran-continues-gulf-attacks) beside Dubai International Airport.

The escalation chain ([CSIS](https://www.csis.org/analysis/irans-war-strategy-dont-calibrate-escalate), [Wikipedia — 2026 Iran war](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2026_Iran_war)):

- 28 February 2026: joint US-Israeli strikes on Iran
- Iran retaliates, expanding the geography of strikes to civilian Gulf infrastructure
- Gulf states absorb physical damage — airports, desalination plants, energy facilities
- Global supply chains fracture: Strait of Hormuz traffic drops from 129 daily ship transits to near zero
- Brent crude surges to $120/barrel
- [19,000+ flight cancellations](https://dronexl.co/2026/03/16/dubai-airport-drone-strike-war/) in the first week
- Pharmaceuticals, electronics, perishable goods — delays worldwide

### The Systemic Pattern

This is not a narrative with heroes and villains. It is a **system archetype** ([System Archetypes — Daniel H. Kim](https://thesystemsthinker.com/wp-content/uploads/2016/03/Systems-Archetypes-Basics-WB002E.pdf)), simultaneously reproducing three traps described by Donella Meadows:

**1. Escalation.** Each side takes action to improve its position — provoking a larger response, which provokes a still larger counter-response. US and Israeli government policy leads to strikes on Iran. Iranian government policy leads to retaliatory strikes against the Gulf. Both sides frame their actions as existential defence. No exit, because "[both sides believe they are fighting existential threats](https://www.csis.org/analysis/irans-war-strategy-dont-calibrate-escalate)."

**2. Shifting the burden.** Decisions to strike are made by some; the physical consequences are borne by others. US military policy initiates operations, but the infrastructure damage falls on Gulf states — third parties drawn into a conflict not of their making. The Iranian government, unable to match its adversaries in conventional military capability, deliberately "[internationalises the costs of war by striking Gulf energy, transport, and civilian infrastructure](https://www.csis.org/analysis/irans-war-strategy-dont-calibrate-escalate)."

**3. Tragedy of the commons.** The shared global resource being degraded is not fisheries or forests. It is international aviation and maritime infrastructure, stable energy supply, and the diplomatic trust required for world trade. Each actor pursues its "rational" interest while the commons deteriorate for everyone.

**The central question:** What would the world look like if participants — states, alliances, institutions — possessed the maturity to see the system, not merely their own position within it?

## 2. Systems Thinking in Geopolitics

### 2.1 Donella Meadows: Where to Intervene in a System

Donella Meadows' landmark 1997 essay "[Leverage Points: Places to Intervene in a System](https://donellameadows.org/archives/leverage-points-places-to-intervene-in-a-system/)" was inspired by attending a NAFTA meeting, where she realised: "a big new system was being proposed but the mechanisms for managing it were inadequate." The framework identifies 12 leverage points — from weak to powerful.

**Weak levers (where 95% of political attention goes):**
- Parameters: sanction volumes, arms deal amounts, aid packages
- Buffer sizes, material flows
- Delays in information and response

**Medium levers:**
- Negative feedback loops (UN Security Council, international law — weakening)
- Positive feedback loops (arms races, escalation spirals — strengthening)
- Information flow structures (who knows what and when — severely distorted)

**Powerful levers (where almost no attention goes):**
- System rules: incentives, penalties, constraints — who writes the rules?
- Self-organisation: can the system generate new structures to respond to crisis?
- System goals: what is the system actually optimised for?

**The most powerful levers:**
- **Paradigms** — the shared assumptions from which everything else follows. Meadows, quoting Emerson: "Every nation and every man instantly surround themselves with a material apparatus which exactly corresponds to their state of thinking" ([donellameadows.org](https://donellameadows.org/archives/leverage-points-places-to-intervene-in-a-system/)).
- **The ability to transcend paradigms** — recognising that no paradigm holds absolute truth. "When you realise that all paradigms are limited, you can choose the one that will help achieve your purpose."

**Application to the current conflict:** Nearly all diplomatic effort concentrates on parameters — ceasefire terms, sanction levels, aid volumes. Almost nobody is working on the paradigm: that security is achieved through military dominance rather than shared infrastructure; that sovereignty means the right to project force rather than the responsibility to maintain shared systems.

### 2.2 System Archetypes in Proxy Wars

The pattern of proxy wars maps precisely onto Meadows' system traps ([Thinking in Systems, PDF](https://research.fit.edu/media/site-specific/researchfitedu/coast-climate-adaptation-library/climate-communications/psychology-amp-behavior/Meadows-2008.-Thinking-in-Systems.pdf)):

**Escalation archetype**: "Side A takes action to improve its position, which causes Side B to respond in kind, which causes Side A to intensify its actions, and so on." Kinetic strikes provoke counter-strikes; expansion of geography leads to attacks on civilian infrastructure, then economic warfare. Each step is "rational" within the cycle; the cycle itself is insane.

**Shifting the burden to the intervener**: "The corrective feedback within the system cannot maintain the desired state. A well-meaning and effective intervener observes the struggle and intervenes." The intervener (US military presence in the Gulf) provides immediate security but undermines the development of regional capacity for self-governance and conflict resolution. For decades "Gulf territories remained largely insulated from direct Iranian fire" — not because the problem was solved, but because the symptom was suppressed.

**Key insight**: Proxy wars are not aberrations. They are the predictable output of a system designed to externalise costs and concentrate benefits. The country initiating strikes does not bear the infrastructure damage. The country absorbing the damage did not make the strategic decision. The civilians whose flights are cancelled, whose water supply is threatened, whose economy is disrupted — had no voice in any of it.

For anyone who watched Brexit upend settled arrangements overnight — pension transfers frozen, healthcare reciprocity questioned, property rights suddenly uncertain — the mechanism is familiar. Strategic decisions taken in Westminster reverberated through the lives of millions of British citizens living abroad who had no ballot paper for the referendum that reshaped their world. Burden-shifting is not an exotic concept; it is lived experience.

### 2.3 Schmachtenberger's Multipolar Trap

Daniel Schmachtenberger's concept of the [multipolar trap](https://stephenreid.net/k/daniel/terms/multipolar%20trap) provides the game-theoretic framing: "A situation in which multiple rational agents, each acting in their own self-interest, produce a collectively destructive outcome for all."

Key characteristics:
- Every actor understands that cooperation would produce a better result
- But the incentive structure compels continued destructive behaviour
- Fear of being outcompeted prevents unilateral restraint
- The result is a "[race to the bottom](https://conversational-leadership.net/multipolar-trap/)," degrading the environment for everyone

Schmachtenberger's examples, directly parallel to the current conflict:
- **Arms races**: developing ever more dangerous capabilities because restraint = vulnerability
- **Tragedy of the commons**: over-exploitation of shared resources (in this case, regional stability)
- **GDP races**: competing for economic dominance, correlated with energy consumption

Proposed exits ([Civilisation Emerging](https://civilizationemerging.com/media/the-metacrisis-with-daniel-schmachtenberger-part-1/)):
1. **Institutional binding**: international frameworks constraining everyone simultaneously
2. **Competing through transparency**: "a race to the top on transparency instead of a race to the bottom"
3. **Game-theory reorientation**: solving classes of multipolar traps universally, not one by one
4. **Simultaneous collective agreements**: all major players commit at once, removing first-mover disadvantage

The fundamental problem: "If there is not a fully global government, law at the level of the nation-state leaves states in a multipolar trap with each other."

## 3. Cooperation Models That Actually Worked

The argument that "this is just how the world works" collapses when you look at historical cases where former enemies chose shared institutions over blame-shifting.

### 3.1 The Marshall Plan (1948-1952): The Joint Venture Model

The [Marshall Plan](https://www.archives.gov/milestone-documents/marshall-plan) was not charity. It was structured as a **joint venture** between the US and European nations with shared responsibility for outcomes.

Key design principles ([Congressional Research Service](https://www.everycrsreport.com/reports/R45079.html)):
- **Not a plan, but a challenge**: The US did not impose a blueprint for recovery. It invited European leaders to coordinate among themselves and create their own plan, which the US would then fund. Agency rested with the recipients.
- **Peer pressure**: The European OEEC "generated peer pressure that encouraged individual countries to live up to their Marshall Plan commitments."
- **Joint management**: Funds were managed jointly by local governments and the US Economic Cooperation Administration. Neither side had unilateral control.
- **Conditioned on cooperation**: Aid was contingent on European cooperation. Countries were compelled to work together to access resources.
- **Result**: The plan "stimulated European economic integration through massive aid packages conditioned on cooperation, and this forced cooperation together with security guarantees provided rapprochement and trust."

**How this differs from current approaches**: The Marshall Plan treated recipients as partners with agency, not as pawns in a geopolitical game. Cooperation was a precondition, not an afterthought. What was built were shared institutions, not dependency.

British readers may recognise a painful echo here. The post-war settlement that produced the Marshall Plan and eventually the European Communities was a project Britain helped inspire but then spent decades oscillating about joining. When it finally left in 2020, one of the core discoveries was how deeply intertwined British prosperity had become with those shared institutions — precisely the structural interdependence Schuman designed for. Disentangling turned out to be far more costly than anyone on the Leave campaign had modelled.

### 3.2 The Schuman Declaration and the Formation of the EU (1950-present): Making War "Materially Impossible"

The most radical cooperation experiment in history began with a single insight. On 9 May 1950, French Foreign Minister Robert Schuman [proposed](https://european-union.europa.eu/principles-countries-history/history-eu/1945-59/schuman-declaration-may-1950_en) placing the coal and steel production of France and **West Germany** — the materials of war — under a single authority.

His words: "The solidarity in production thus established will make it plain that any war between France and Germany becomes not merely unthinkable, but materially impossible" ([The Conversation — 75th anniversary](https://theconversation.com/the-birth-of-the-eu-europe-celebrates-75th-anniversary-of-the-schuman-declaration-256338)).

The brilliance of the design:
- It did not ask former enemies to forgive and forget
- It did not rely on moral persuasion or shared values
- It made war **structurally impossible** by intertwining the material bases of military capability
- France and Germany — who had fought three wars between 1870 and 1945 — were placed in a system where their economic interests were literally inseparable

The Treaty of Paris (1951) established the ECSC among six countries. This expanded into the EEC (1958), then into the European Union. From coal and steel to a common currency, open borders, and shared institutions for 450 million people.

**The key lesson**: Shared institutions do not require shared values or trust as a precondition. They **create** trust as a byproduct of structural interdependence. Schuman did not wait for France and Germany to like each other. He made their economic survival dependent on working together.

For a British audience, this carries a particular irony. The UK sat out the ECSC, joined the EEC decades late, and then voted to leave — an experiment in un-intertwining that has produced exactly the frictions the Schuman model predicted: trade barriers, regulatory divergence, reduced diplomatic leverage. The lesson is not that Brexit was "wrong" — sovereign decisions are sovereign decisions — but that disentangling from shared institutions has measurable costs that were systematically underestimated.

### 3.3 ASEAN: The Consensus Model (since 1967)

Southeast Asia in 1967 was described as "the Balkans of the East" — a region of deep enmity and distrust. The creation of ASEAN was an experiment: could small and medium states manage conflict without external intervention?

Core principles of the "ASEAN Way" ([USIP](https://www.usip.org/sites/default/files/2020-08/sr_477-built_for_trust_not_for_conflict_asean_faces_the_future-sr.pdf), [East Asia Forum](https://eastasiaforum.org/2023/12/06/asean-centrality-still-key-to-managing-regional-conflicts/)):
- Non-interference in internal affairs
- Quiet diplomacy over public confrontation
- Non-use of force
- **Decision-making through consensus** — no voting, no majoritarian overruling

The result: "ASEAN's collective efforts and culture of dialogue and consensus-building ended distrust and set in motion a remarkable cycle of peace, trust, and prosperity."

Limitations (important to acknowledge):
- Consensus can mean "sweeping conflicts under the carpet"
- ASEAN's High Council for dispute resolution has **never been invoked**
- The Myanmar crisis exposed the limits of non-interference
- Better at preventing new conflicts than resolving existing ones

**What this proves**: A region with deep historical enmity can build working cooperation without a hegemonic enforcer. But consensus alone is not sufficient — structural mechanisms are needed.

The contrast with the Commonwealth is instructive. The Commonwealth of Nations, which many British expats instinctively reference as a cooperation model, operates on similar principles — voluntary membership, consensus, non-interference. But it lacks the structural binding that gives ASEAN or the EU teeth. It is a network of goodwill rather than a framework of interdependence. The question is whether goodwill alone is adequate when serious interests collide.

### 3.4 The Nordic Model (since 1952): Cooperation Without Coercion

The Nordic model demonstrates cooperation through shared institutions without the pressure of an external threat or economic compulsion ([norden.org](https://www.norden.org/en/information/official-nordic-co-operation)).

Key features:
- Free movement of people since the 1950s (predating the EU's Schengen zone by decades)
- 12 joint Nordic institutions: science, culture, ecology
- Nordic Council (87 representatives, inter-parliamentary cooperation)
- Nordic Council of Ministers (inter-governmental coordination)
- Shared values: strong social welfare systems, consensus-based labour relations, international mediation

International influence: "The internationalism of the Nordic countries is characterised by a shared commitment to international institutions and law, agenda-setting, and building bridges between North and South, East and West."

The difficulty of replication: "The unique degree of shared cultural and social history among the Nordic countries may make it difficult to replicate for other regions." This is an honest caveat — cultural homogeneity lowers the transaction costs of cooperation. But **the principles of institutional design** are transferable.

British expats in Paphos often point to the ease of Nordic cooperation and wonder why the same does not apply more broadly. The answer is instructive: the Nordics invested decades in institutional infrastructure — shared labour markets, mutual recognition of qualifications, joint research programmes — before the cooperation looked "natural." What appears effortless is the product of deliberate design.

### 3.5 Elinor Ostrom's Commons Governance: Proof That Cooperation Works

Elinor Ostrom received the [Nobel Prize in Economics in 2009](https://www.nobelprize.org/prizes/economic-sciences/2009/ostrom/facts/) for demonstrating that **communities can manage shared resources without state regulation or privatisation**. She documented over 800 cases worldwide.

Her 8 design principles for successful commons governance:
1. **Rules adapted to local context** — no one-size-fits-all template
2. **Participatory decision-making** — rules are followed more willingly when people helped write them
3. **Monitoring** — users themselves oversee compliance
4. **Graduated sanctions** — warning before punishment, proportional responses
5. **Conflict resolution mechanisms** — fast, inexpensive, accessible
6. **Recognised rights to self-governance** — external authorities respect local decisions
7. **Nested organisations** — governance at multiple scales, from local to regional
8. **Clear boundaries** — who has rights and responsibilities

Ostrom's work "moved this subject from the margin to the forefront of scientific attention, demonstrating how common resources — forests, fisheries, oil fields, pastures — can be successfully managed by the people who use them, rather than by governments or private companies."

**Application to geopolitics**: The current international system operates as though only two options exist — state sovereignty (privatisation of commons) or world government (centralised regulation). Ostrom proved there is a **third path: polycentric governance** — multiple overlapping institutions with shared responsibility, adapted to context, with graduated enforcement.

This resonates with British constitutional tradition more than it might first appear. The common law system, parish governance, devolution to Scotland, Wales, and Northern Ireland — the UK's own history is one of layered, overlapping jurisdictions that evolved organically. Ostrom formalised what English legal tradition practised for centuries: governance at the scale that fits the problem.

## 4. Group Dynamics and Collective Maturity

### 4.1 Bowen Family Systems Theory: Nations as Families

Murray Bowen developed [family systems theory](https://www.thebowencenter.org/core-concepts-diagrams) in the 1940s-50s, describing patterns that emerge in families under stress. These patterns map onto international relations with uncomfortable precision.

**Triangulation**: When anxiety between two parties becomes unbearable, they pull in a third. "The most uncomfortable member of the dyad predictably draws a third person into the twosome."

Geopolitical parallel: The conflict between US and Iranian military strategies pulls Gulf states into a triangle where they absorb the anxiety (quite literally — missile strikes) of a dyad to which they do not belong. Gulf states become the "triangulated third" — used by both sides to manage their anxiety without addressing the root dyadic conflict.

If you have watched as an expat while both your former country and your new country expect you to "pick a side" — you know the triangulation mechanism at a personal level. British nationals in Cyprus experienced this acutely during Brexit negotiations, simultaneously pressured by London and Brussels while having meaningful ties to both.

**Emotional reactivity vs. differentiation**: "An individual who has differentiated from their family can interact with them without emotional reactivity and can balance emotions and thoughts, resisting the pull of the dysfunctional system."

Nations operating from reactivity: The Iranian government retaliates not strategically but because its identity demands retribution. Israeli leadership frames every threat as existential because the national narrative requires existential framing. US leadership intervenes because the self-concept of global policeman demands intervention. Each nation is **fused** with its role in the system.

A differentiated nation: would respond rather than react; would distinguish genuine existential threats from identity-triggering events; would hold positions without needing the other to change; would refuse to be triangulated into others' conflicts.

**Multigenerational transmission**: Bowen showed that anxiety patterns transmit across generations. The Middle Eastern conflict is a textbook case: grievances, identities, and reaction patterns pass from generation to generation, each inheriting the emotional reactivity of the last.

**What differentiation looks like at national level.** A differentiated nation can:
- Hold its values without requiring other nations to validate them
- Respond to threats proportionally, without identity-driven escalation
- Refuse to be drawn into triangles
- Distinguish between "we are being attacked" (real threat) and "we feel attacked" (emotional reactivity)
- Maintain relationships with perceived adversaries without losing its own sense of self

### 4.2 Peter Senge: Nations as Learning Organisations

Peter Senge's [*The Fifth Discipline*](https://infed.org/mobi/peter-senge-and-the-learning-organization/) describes learning organisations as places "where people continually expand their capacity to create the results they truly desire, where new and expansive patterns of thinking are nurtured, where collective aspiration is set free."

His five disciplines, applied to international relations:

1. **Systems thinking** (the cornerstone): seeing the entire system, not individual events. Currently each nation sees only its piece of the puzzle. A "learning international community" would see feedback loops, unintended consequences, and interdependencies.

2. **Personal mastery**: individual nations developing genuine competence rather than projecting strength through military posturing.

3. **Mental models**: surfacing and interrogating assumptions that drive policy. What mental models lead the Iranian government to view escalation as the only option? What mental models lead US leadership to believe military intervention produces stability?

4. **Shared vision**: "When dialogue is joined with systems thinking, there is the possibility of creating a language more suited for dealing with complexity." Currently there is no shared vision — only competing visions imposed by force.

5. **Team learning**: "A group of people is smarter than one or two smart people." Collective intelligence applied to conflict resolution rather than bilateral power games.

Senge's later work "centred on advancing shared understanding of complex issues and shared leadership for healthier human systems, including large cross-sector projects on global food systems, climate change, and regenerative economics." The framework exists. Its application to geopolitics remains almost entirely theoretical.

### 4.3 Johan Galtung: Structural Violence and Positive Peace

Johan Galtung's distinction between **negative peace** (absence of direct violence) and **positive peace** (presence of social justice and cooperation) is critical for understanding why "peace agreements" fail time after time ([Transcend](https://www.transcend.org/tms/2024/07/johan-galtung-positive-and-negative-peace/), [Galtung 1969](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/002234336900600301)).

**Structural violence**: "Violence occurs when people are influenced so that their actual physical and mental realisations are below their potential realisations." This includes economic exploitation, political marginalisation, and institutional oppression — the conditions that create the grievances on which proxy wars feed.

**Three types of violence (interconnected)**:
1. **Direct violence**: drone strikes, bombardments, military operations
2. **Structural violence**: economic sanctions, unequal resource distribution, institutional power imbalances
3. **Cultural violence**: narratives that justify direct and structural violence — "they are terrorists," "it is the only language they understand," "we have no choice"

**Galtung's resolution framework**:
- **Peacekeeping**: stop the immediate violence (ceasefires, peacekeeping forces)
- **Peacemaking**: address the surface issues of the conflict (negotiations, mediation)
- **Peacebuilding**: address root causes — structural and cultural violence

Most international efforts stop at peacekeeping. In the current Middle East conflict, even that has not been achieved. Peacebuilding — addressing the structural conditions that make war rational for each actor — is absent from the agenda.

**Positive peace as cooperation**: Galtung defines positive peace as "cooperation and integration between human societies." Not merely the absence of bombs, but the presence of shared institutions, fair resource distribution, and mutual recognition.

The NHS provides an unexpected illustration. Britain's National Health Service embodies Galtung's positive peace at domestic level: healthcare as a shared institution, universally accessible, funded collectively. Cyprus's GeSY system, modelled loosely on similar principles, does the same. The question Galtung poses is why the institutional imagination that produced the NHS or GeSY at national level seems to evaporate at international level.

## 5. "One Boat": Shared Fate

### 5.1 Dubai Airport as Proof of Interconnection

The strike on Dubai's airport is a case study in shared fate ([DroneXL](https://dronexl.co/2026/03/16/dubai-airport-drone-strike-war/), [GetTransport](https://blog.gettransport.com/news/global-airline-cancellations-impact-logistics/)):

- A $30,000 drone paralysed the world's busiest international airport
- 9,500+ flights cancelled across the region in 5 days
- Strait of Hormuz traffic collapsed from 129 daily ship transits to near zero
- Freight rates surged globally; transit times increased by weeks
- Pharmaceuticals, electronics, perishable goods — delays worldwide
- Second-order effects hit India, Europe, Japan, South Korea through supply chain disruption

As one analyst noted: "The very success of Dubai's logistics ecosystem made it a strategic target. The emirate's ports, airports, free zones, and financial infrastructure together form one of the world's most critical logistics nodes. Its disruption reverberates across global trade."

**The lesson**: In an interconnected world there is no such thing as a "regional" conflict. When you strike a fuel tank beside an airport connecting 110 countries, you strike the global system. The bomber frames it as a strike against an enemy; the system experiences it as self-harm.

British expats understand this concretely. Dubai is the primary transit hub between the UK and much of Asia, Australia, and East Africa. Thousands of British business travellers, retirees visiting family in Australia, and remote workers commuting between London and South-East Asian cities route through DXB. The strike did not merely disrupt Emirati operations — it severed a critical artery in the travel infrastructure that British expatriate life depends on. Add that many UK firms run Middle Eastern operations out of Dubai, and the "regional" label dissolves entirely.

### 5.2 COVID-19: A Cooperation Test Humanity Failed

The pandemic was the most recent test of "one boat" thinking. Humanity failed it comprehensively.

**Vaccine nationalism**: Wealthy nations reserved enough doses to vaccinate their populations several times over, leaving less for others. The cost of this policy was calculated: "[up to $9.2 trillion](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9519117/)" in global economic losses if developing economies are denied vaccine access.

**Structural failure of COVAX**: The multilateral initiative for equitable vaccine distribution "[had no incentives for high-income country participation or enforcement mechanisms](https://www.who.int/initiatives/act-accelerator/covax)." It did not deliver even half its target — 2 billion doses in 2021.

**Systemic insight**: Even from the standpoint of pure self-interest, vaccine nationalism was irrational. Variants that evolved in unvaccinated populations spread globally, prolonging the pandemic for everyone. The "me first" approach was "self-defeating because no country can solve this alone."

**What this reveals about civilisational maturity**: Faced with a shared existential threat (a novel virus), the dominant response was: grab resources, protect your own, leave the rest to fend for themselves. This is not adult behaviour in a crisis. It is children fighting for lifeboat seats while the ship sinks.

The UK's experience was illustrative in both directions. The vaccine rollout domestically was genuinely impressive — rapid procurement, efficient NHS distribution, high uptake. But the export controls, the resistance to patent waivers, and the slow COVAX contributions demonstrated exactly the tension Schmachtenberger describes: domestically rational, globally destructive.

### 5.3 Planetary Boundaries: The Ultimate "One Boat"

In January 2024, 22 leading international researchers published a "[Planetary Commons](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2024/01/240122182828.htm)" framework in PNAS, arguing that critical Earth systems must be governed as shared resources.

**The science**: Earth has nine planetary boundaries defining a "safe operating space" for humanity. As of 2023, [six of nine have been crossed](https://www.science.org/doi/10.1126/sciadv.adh2458). We are "deep in the danger zone, putting ourselves at greater risk of crossing tipping points that could destabilise the planet" ([Stockholm Resilience Centre](https://www.stockholmresilience.org/research/planetary-boundaries.html)).

**The governance gap**: "Despite their interdependence, none of these agreements or goals will be achieved without success in the others, yet work on them proceeds in silos, which slows progress."

**The Planetary Commons proposal**:
- Earth's tipping elements (ice sheets, tropical forests, ocean circulation, carbon sinks) must be governed as global commons
- These commons "are often located within sovereign territories" — requiring a rethinking of sovereignty
- A "nested approach to Earth system governance" with commitments at multiple scales
- "Overarching governance commitments aimed at protecting Earth-regulating systems in a just and inclusive manner"

**The fundamental challenge**: This requires "adapting and adjusting notions of state sovereignty and self-determination" — precisely what nations resist most. The planetary boundaries framework says: your sovereignty over your rainforest is limited by the fact that the entire planet's climate depends on it.

For a country that built an empire on the principle of sovereignty — and then dismantled it partly on the same principle — the tension is familiar. British political thought has oscillated for centuries between sovereignty-as-autonomy and sovereignty-as-responsibility. The planetary boundaries framework forces the question: at what point does a state's sovereign right to exploit its territory conflict with the rest of humanity's right to a habitable planet?

## 6. Psychological Frameworks for Collective Maturity

### 6.1 Robert Kegan: Where Is Humanity on the Developmental Scale?

Robert Kegan's constructive-developmental theory identifies five stages of adult development (plus stage 0 — infancy, where there is no differentiation between self and environment). Applied to nations and civilisations, it works as a diagnostic framework ([Natali Morad — Kegan's Theory](https://medium.com/@NataliMorad/how-to-be-an-adult-kegans-theory-of-adult-development-d63f4311b553), [Alive and Thriving](https://aliveandthriving.substack.com/p/kegans-theory-of-development-framework)):

**Stage 1 — The Impulsive Mind (impulse and perception)**: Actions determined by immediate impulses. No capacity to take another's perspective. Magical thinking — actions have no consequences beyond the present moment. In a child, this is age 2-6. At national level: a state acting from pure impulse without modelling consequences. Cannot see any perspective other than its own. Believes in magical outcomes: "if we just hit hard enough, the problem will disappear." The inability to hold two perspectives simultaneously means every threat triggers immediate reaction rather than response. No strategy — only reflexes.

**Stage 2 — The Imperial Mind (self-interest)**: "The self is defined by its needs, interests, and desires." Nations at this stage operate from pure self-interest, treating others as means to ends. Alliances are transactional. Cooperation lasts exactly as long as it serves immediate interest. Progress from Stage 1: the actor CAN model consequences and others' perspectives, but only instrumentally — "what do I get out of this?"

**Stage 3 — The Socialised Mind (identity through group)**: "What is most important is the ideas, norms, and beliefs of the people in the systems around us." Nations at this stage derive identity from alliances, blocs, and ideological groupings (NATO, "axis of resistance," "the free world"). Cannot question the group's narrative without feeling existentially threatened.

**Stage 4 — The Self-Authoring Mind (internal compass)**: "We prioritise defining who we are and step back from being defined by others." A self-authoring nation would have a clear sense of values and interests independent of alliance dynamics. Could disagree with allies without existential anxiety. Could cooperate with adversaries without identity crisis.

**Stage 5 — The Self-Transforming Mind (holding complexity)**: "Even the self-authored identity and internal value system are no longer fixed. Instead of being subject to a particular identity or ideology, the individual regards them as objects — tools that can be used, questioned, refined, or discarded." Research suggests only ~1% of adults reach this stage.

**Diagnosis**: Most nations operate at **Stage 2-3** — oscillating between naked self-interest and tribal identity. The Iranian government's response is Stage 2 (self-interest calculation) wrapped in Stage 3 (identity narrative of resistance). Israeli leadership's existential framing is Stage 3 (identity inseparable from the threat narrative). US leadership's intervention is Stage 2 (strategic interest) disguised as Stage 4 (values-driven action).

For British expats, Kegan's stages are not abstract. Emigrating is quite literally a change of perspective. Seeing your country from outside, for the first time. Discovering that "we" and "they" are constructs. Watching from Paphos as Westminster debates play out and realising the narrative looks different from the outside. This is Kegan's development in action — the transition from Stage 3 to Stage 4: from "I am my context" to "I can observe my context."

**What Stage 4 would look like for nations**: A nation capable of honestly naming its real interests, separate from ideological framing. Capable of cooperating with adversaries where interests align without treating cooperation as betrayal. Possessing an internal value system that does not depend on the existence of an enemy.

**What Stage 5 would look like**: A nation capable of questioning its own narrative. Capable of acknowledging that its identity is a construct: useful, but not absolute. Capable of holding the perspective that the "enemy" has legitimate interests without losing its own position. This is what Kegan means by "self-transformation" — the ability to see one's own operating system as one option among many.

### 6.2 Spiral Dynamics / Clare Graves: Levels of Civilisational Development

Clare Graves' developmental model (from 1952), popularised as Spiral Dynamics by Beck and Cowan, describes eight levels of increasingly complex value systems ([Wikipedia — Spiral Dynamics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spiral_Dynamics), [Wikipedia — Clare W. Graves](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Clare_W._Graves)).

Levels relevant to geopolitical analysis:

- **RED (Power/impulsive)**: Might makes right. Domination through force. "I want, I take." Much of proxy warfare operates at this level beneath sophisticated camouflage.
- **BLUE (Order/purposeful)**: Rules, authority, the one true way. Religious nationalism, ideological certainty. The Iranian government's theocratic framing, American exceptionalism.
- **ORANGE (Achievement/strategic)**: Success, competition, individual achievement. The "realist" school of international relations. Rational self-interest, strategic calculation.
- **GREEN (Communitarian/egalitarian)**: Consensus, equality, human rights, environmental concern. EU aspirations, the Nordic model, international human rights frameworks.
- **YELLOW (Integrative/systemic)**: Systems thinking, integration of all previous levels, understanding that different contexts require different approaches. Very few institutions operate here consistently.
- **TURQUOISE (Holistic/global)**: Seeing the entire planet as a single system. Planetary commons thinking.

**Practical application**: Beck and Cowan were involved in assisting Nelson Mandela in navigating South Africa's transition from apartheid — essentially helping a society move from RED/BLUE dynamics to GREEN/ORANGE without the destructive collapse that typically accompanies such transitions.

**Important caveat**: Spiral Dynamics "[lacks generally accepted academic validity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spiral_Dynamics)," though it has been applied in management consulting and some political contexts. Its value is as a thinking tool, not as a scientific framework.

**The insight it provides**: Different nations and actors in the current conflict are literally operating from different developmental levels. Attempting to negotiate between actors at different levels is like a conversation between a nursery child and a postgraduate student on the same topic. They do not disagree on facts — they operate from fundamentally different meaning-making systems.

### 6.3 "Shifting the Burden" at the Psychological Level

Meadows' "shifting the burden" archetype has a direct psychological parallel. At the individual level:
- Painkillers for chronic pain (instead of addressing the cause)
- Debt for chronic overspending (instead of changing spending patterns)
- Caffeine for chronic sleep deprivation (instead of sleeping)

At the civilisational level:
- Military force for chronic insecurity (instead of building shared security architecture)
- Sanctions for economic disputes (instead of restructuring trade relationships)
- Proxy wars for geopolitical competition (instead of developing cooperative frameworks)
- Blame-shifting for policy failures (instead of accepting shared responsibility)

In every case, "the intervention can become a systemic trap. The intervener quickly brings the system to the state everyone wants" ([Meadows](https://research.fit.edu/media/site-specific/researchfitedu/coast-climate-adaptation-library/climate-communications/psychology-amp-behavior/Meadows-2008.-Thinking-in-Systems.pdf)) — but the underlying problem worsens, and the system becomes dependent on the intervention.

## 7. Contemporary Thinkers on Civilisational Maturity

### 7.1 Daniel Schmachtenberger: The Metacrisis

Schmachtenberger frames the totality of existential risks (climate, AI, nuclear weapons, biological threats) as the **metacrisis** — not separate problems, but interconnected manifestations of a single coordination failure ([Civilisation Emerging](https://civilizationemerging.com/media/the-metacrisis-with-daniel-schmachtenberger-part-1/)).

His hierarchy of crises:
1. **Surface crises**: climate, nuclear weapons, AI risks (symptoms)
2. **Systemic crises**: economics, politics (structures producing the symptoms)
3. **Epistemological crises**: truth, meaning-making (breakdown of shared understanding)
4. **Meaning crises**: purpose, values (the void driving destructive behaviour)
5. **The metacrisis**: coordination failure itself (the generating function)

His "third attractor" — neither market-driven collapse nor authoritarian control, but "a civilisation that integrates both 'inner' wisdom and 'outer' coordinative capacity at planetary scale."

Key concept: **Moloch** — "[the god of negative-sum competition](https://www.eurekastreet.com.au/our-greatest-threat-isnt-climate-change-or-ai-its-moloch)" or "coordination failures" ([Digital Habitats](https://digitalhabitats.global/blogs/digital-thoughts/misalignment-ai-moloch-daniel-schmachtenberger-and-liv-boeree)). Schmachtenberger uses this metaphor to describe the force pushing rational actors towards collectively irrational outcomes. The strike on Dubai's airport is Moloch in action: each actor's "rational" decision produces an outcome nobody wanted.

### 7.2 Charles Johnston: Cultural Maturity

Johnston, a psychiatrist and futurist, spent 30 years developing the concept of **Cultural Maturity** — arguing that it represents "a critical '[growing up](https://culturalmaturityblog.net/2016/02/humanitys-necessary-growing-up-the-concept-of-cultural-maturity/)' as a species."

The central argument: human civilisation has functioned using culture as a parent — providing absolute rules, clear distinctions of good and evil, certainty about identity and purpose. Cultural Maturity means leaving this parental structure behind and accepting adult responsibility for navigating complexity.

Specific changes Cultural Maturity demands:
- **Moving beyond "us vs. them"**: Transcending chosen-people/evil-enemy dynamics. Not as a moral aspiration, but as a survival necessity — "this is not just ideology, but a condition for survival in the presence of weapons of mass destruction."
- **Accepting real limits**: a mature relationship with death, planetary boundaries, human capacity for harm
- **Systems thinking**: not mechanistic engineering, but thinking that "reflects that we are alive — and human"
- **Integrative meta-perspective**: holding multiple viewpoints without collapsing into relativism or rigidity

Johnston's answer to "why this is not just idealism": Cultural Maturity is "the only game in town" because continuing historical escalation patterns with modern weaponry leads to extinction. He points to evidence that the shift is already beginning: the fall of the Berlin Wall, ecological consciousness, the evolution of relationship structures. The question is whether it happens quickly enough.

For a society steeped in the Enlightenment tradition, the concept of Cultural Maturity is simultaneously familiar and challenging. Britain's political tradition — from Magna Carta through the Glorious Revolution to devolution — is itself a story of incremental maturation: each step expanding the circle of those whose perspective must be held. Johnston is asking whether that process can now extend to the planetary scale.

### 7.3 Yuval Noah Harari: Shared Myths and Information Networks

Harari's central insight from *Sapiens*: "[Any large-scale human cooperation — whether a modern state, a medieval church, an ancient city, or an archaic tribe — is rooted in shared myths that exist only in people's collective imagination](https://www.ynharari.com/book/nexus/)."

The mechanism of cooperation: "Belief in the same fictions or stories about how the world works allows people who would otherwise neither know nor trust each other to cooperate on a massive scale."

In his 2024 book *Nexus*, Harari explores how information networks shape societies: "Humankind gains power by building large networks of cooperation, but the easiest way to build and maintain these networks is by spreading fictions, fantasies, and mass delusions."

**Application to the current crisis**: Every side in the Middle East conflict is sustained by a shared myth — about who they are, who the enemy is, what justice demands. These myths enable internal cooperation (national unity, alliance cohesion) but prevent cooperation across boundaries. The myths are not "wrong" in any simple sense — they are the narrative infrastructure holding each society together. The problem is that **competing myths are currently organised to produce escalation, not cooperation**.

Harari's warning about AI applies here too: new information networks may "prevent future generations from even trying to expose lies and fictions." If AI-driven propaganda locks each population deeper into its narrative, the capacity for cross-border cooperation shrinks further.

### 7.4 Charles Eisenstein: The Story of Interbeing

Eisenstein frames humanity's predicament as a narrative crisis. We live in the "Story of Separation" — a worldview holding that we are fundamentally separate beings competing for limited resources. Everything from capitalism to warfare to environmental destruction follows from this story ([Wikipedia — Charles Eisenstein](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charles_Eisenstein)).

The alternative is the "Story of Interbeing" — a term borrowed from Thich Nhat Hanh — which recognises that "everything is interconnected and interdependent."

From *Sacred Economics*: "The economy of the future will call forth the gifts of each of us; will emphasise cooperation over competition; will encourage circulation over accumulation; and will be cyclical, not linear."

This is not mysticism. It is a structural claim: the current economic and political system is designed around the assumption of separateness. If the assumption is wrong (and physics, ecology, and supply chain analysis say it is), then the system produces pathological outcomes — including wars that damage the attacker as much as the target.

### 7.5 Otto Scharmer: Theory U and Ecosystem Awareness

Scharmer's [Theory U](https://www.presencing.org/theoryu) framework describes how leaders and systems can shift from "ego-system" awareness (focus on individual/institutional self-interest) to "eco-system" awareness (focus on the wellbeing of the whole).

His key distinction: "Meeting the challenges of this century requires updating our economic logic and operating system — from an obsolete 'ego-system' fully focused on personal wellbeing to an eco-system awareness that emphasises the wellbeing of the whole."

Practical application: from 2018 to 2024, the UN's SDG Leadership Labs were conducted in 26 countries using Theory U principles to "strengthen cooperation, innovation capacity, and systems leadership."

### 7.6 Zak Stein: Education as the Root

Stein argues that all civilisational crises are rooted in education: "[If you are looking for the root — education is the root](https://www.zakstein.org/publications.html). All other crises are basically crises of human decision-making. The root of decision-making is in how that person was educated."

He describes the current moment as "a major world-historical moment of transformation in which the old world is passing away and a new one has not yet emerged." The question is whether we can educate people capable of navigating the transition.

### 7.7 Nora Bateson: Warm Data and Transcontextual Understanding

Bateson (daughter of Gregory Bateson, a pioneer of systems thinking) introduces the concept of "[Warm Data](https://batesoninstitute.org/warm-data/)" — "transcontextual information about the relationships that integrate a complex system."

Her argument: quantitative data ("cold data") alone cannot capture the relational, contextual nature of complex systems. You cannot understand a conflict by counting missiles and casualties. You need to understand the relationships, narratives, historical patterns, and emotional dynamics — the "warm data" — that give cold data meaning.

"Complex systemic challenges are formed in the interaction of multiple contexts — economy, nature, art, culture, technology — and to effectively address such challenges we need to understand the transcontextual interdependence."

### 7.8 Jonathan Haidt: Moral Psychology and Tribal Dynamics

Haidt's research shows that humans are "[90% chimp but also 10% bee](https://jonathanhaidt.com/politics/) — evolved to unite for the good of the hive." Our moral sense evolved to enable within-group cooperation, but it simultaneously creates between-group hostility.

The mechanism: "Our moral sense actually evolved to bind groups into teams that can cooperate to compete with other teams" ([AEI](https://www.aei.org/articles/the-tribal-mind-moral-reasoning-and-public-discourse/)). This is precisely the dynamic playing out in the current conflict — each side internally united by moral certainty in its rightness and the other's wickedness.

Haidt's practical proposal: "Mix up the teams" — create situations where people with different views are forced to work together for shared goals. At the international level this means creating projects so vital that cooperation becomes necessary regardless of political affiliation. Climate change, pandemic preparedness, asteroid defence — all candidates. The problem: these shared threats have so far proved insufficient to overcome tribal dynamics.

British political culture offers a domestic illustration. The wartime coalition government of 1940-1945 — Churchill, Attlee, Bevin, Beveridge — brought together people who profoundly disagreed about nearly everything in order to address a shared existential threat. The result was not only victory but the creation of the NHS, the welfare state, and the framework that sustained Britain for decades. Haidt's question is whether the same mechanism can operate at global scale without requiring an existential war to trigger it.

## 8. Synthesis: What "Growing Up" Would Look Like in Practice

Drawing on all the frameworks above — not as utopian aspiration, but as an engineering challenge.

### 8.1 Structural Changes ("Hardware")

**1. The Schuman Principle, applied globally.** Make war materially impossible by intertwining critical infrastructure. If a strike on Dubai's airport disrupted Iranian trade to the same extent as Emirati trade — the calculus changes. This is not idealism; it is engineering. The EU did it with coal and steel.

**2. Ostrom's polycentric governance.** Replace the binary of "state sovereignty vs. world government" with nested, overlapping governance structures for shared resources. The Planetary Commons framework ([PNAS, 2024](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2024/01/240122182828.htm)) is a starting point: ice sheets, tropical forests, ocean circulation, governed as commons with graduated sanctions and participatory decision-making.

**3. Mandatory cost internalisation.** Currently, some states' military policies lead to strikes in the Middle East without bearing any infrastructure costs. Gulf states absorb the damage. Civilians lose their flights. A mature system would require every actor to internalise the costs of its actions — including second- and third-order effects on third parties.

**4. Information transparency.** Meadows identified information flows as leverage point #6 — "highly effective and relatively cheap to modify." If every nation's military spending, arms sales, proxy funding, and strategic objectives were transparent, the multipolar trap weakens. You cannot race to the bottom if everyone can see the race.

### 8.2 Cognitive Changes ("Software")

**5. Paradigm shift from security-through-dominance to security-through-interdependence.** This is Meadows' leverage point #2. The current paradigm: "I am secure when I am stronger than my enemies." The alternative: "I am secure when my enemies' wellbeing depends on mine and mine on theirs." The EU is proof that this works.

**6. Differentiation (Bowen).** Nations capable of holding their positions without emotional reactivity. Responding to provocations with strategic calm rather than identity-driven escalation. Refusing to be triangulated into others' conflicts.

**7. Self-authoring (Kegan's Stage 4) as a minimum.** Nations with genuinely internal value systems rather than identities defined through opposition to an enemy. Most nations today are at Stage 3 — their identity is inseparable from their group (alliance, ideology, religion). A Stage 4 nation could cooperate with adversaries on shared interests without existential crisis.

**8. Systems literacy.** The ability to see feedback loops, unintended consequences, and interdependencies. Currently policymakers optimise for direct effects, ignoring systemic dynamics. A systems-literate policy apparatus would ask: "What are the second- and third-order consequences of this strike? Who absorbs the costs we are externalising?"

### 8.3 Narrative Changes ("The Meaning Layer")

**9. From "chosen people vs. evil enemy" to "participants in a shared fate."** Johnston's Cultural Maturity framework. Not a request to abandon identity, but a recognition: identity defined through opposition is a developmental stage, not an endpoint.

**10. From competing myths to shared myths (Harari).** Current myths enable within-group cooperation but prevent cross-border cooperation. New myths are needed that enable cooperation at planetary scale. Climate change could be such a myth — "we are all on one planet" — but it has not yet achieved the narrative power of national identity.

**11. From the "Story of Separation" to the "Story of Interbeing" (Eisenstein).** Not a religious or spiritual shift, but a structural recognition: in an interconnected world, harming another IS harming oneself. The strike on Dubai's airport proves this physically — disrupting one node disrupts the entire network.

### 8.4 An Honest Assessment

Is all of this likely to happen in time — before the next Dubai, the next proxy war, the next escalation spiral? Probably not.

The frameworks exist. The evidence is clear. The models are proven (the EU, ASEAN, Nordic cooperation, Ostrom's commons). The thinkers are articulate and precise.

What is missing is not knowledge, but **will**. Specifically, the will to accept short-term costs for long-term stability. Every framework above requires actors to give something up: sovereignty, competitive advantage, the comfort of blame, the simplicity of enemies.

Schmachtenberger's diagnosis is the most honest: the multipolar trap means no single actor can move first without suffering. The solution requires **simultaneous** commitment from all major actors — and that requires trust that does not currently exist.

The EU overcame this through Schuman's brilliance: start small (coal and steel), prove that cooperation works, build trust incrementally, expand scope gradually. But that process took decades, and the current pace of escalation does not allow decades.

The question is not whether humanity **can** grow up. The historical record says it can. The question is whether it will grow up **fast enough** — before the feedback loops of escalation, environmental degradation, and technological risk produce irreversible damage.

Hans Jonas put it most starkly: humanity has become "an unchained Prometheus — possessing god-like technological power without god-like wisdom." His answer was the "imperative of responsibility": Act so that the consequences of your actions do not destroy the possibility of future life.

For those of us living between countries — holding two passports or none, navigating systems designed for people who stay put, watching from Paphos or Limassol or Larnaca as distant decisions reshape our daily infrastructure — the imperative is not abstract. We are already living proof that identity can be plural, that cooperation across difference is not merely theoretical, and that the "one boat" is not a metaphor but a description of Tuesday morning's flight schedule.

That imperative — simple, clear, adult — is the minimum standard of civilisational maturity.
